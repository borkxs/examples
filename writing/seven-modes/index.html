Music Theory - Modes of the Major Scale

The Seven Modes

A field guide to why they sound the way they do - and why key signature alone cannot tell you that.

Key signature is not specific enough. That is the whole problem modes solve, and the whole reason the subject feels cryptic until it suddenly does not.

Seven scales. Same notes. Same key signature on the staff. Yet they sound completely different. A minor scale and a Dorian scale built on the same root share six of their seven notes and would be notated identically in many contexts - but one sounds like Santana and the other sounds like Bach. The map is not the territory, and the staff is not the sound.

What we are really after is a way to describe the sound - specifically, how each mode's particular arrangement of intervals produces a distinct emotional character. The shorthand is: measure each mode against the major scale, and note what is flatted.

1. Starting from C major

The modes of the major scale are what you get if you generalize the idea of a relative minor to cover all seven notes. A minor is C major's relative minor - same notes, different starting point. There are five more starting points we have not named yet.

Starting on each successive note of C major while keeping all the same pitches produces seven distinct scales. Click a row to inspect it, or press the play button to hear the ascending scale.

Seeing them side by side makes the key-signature problem concrete. All seven would be written with no sharps or flats on a staff. The notation is identical. The sound is not.

2. Measuring deviation from major

To compare modes systematically, measure each one against its own major scale - not against C major, but against the major scale starting on the same root. D Dorian vs. D major. E Phrygian vs. E major. This normalizes the comparison.

D major has two sharps: F♯ and C♯. D Dorian, built from C major's notes, has neither. Those missing sharps become flatted scale degrees: the 3rd (F) and the 7th (C). Flat the 3rd and 7th of any major scale and you get Dorian.

This is not specific to D. Flat the 3rd and 7th of G major and you get G Dorian. Flat the 3rd and 7th of B♭ major and you get B♭ Dorian. The relationship is universal - which is why it is useful.

Listed out, the seven modes have these deviations from their respective major scales. The pattern is hard to see from this ordering - Lydian's sharp and Locrian's five flats do not obviously belong to the same system.

3. Why this sequence matters

That sequence - 7-3-6-2-5 - contains the jazz subsequences you already know: 2-5-1 and 3-6-2-5-1. It is not a coincidence. The circle of fourths is the geometry of cadential tension.

The circle below shows all 12 major keys arranged by fifths. The toggle unrolls it - the same motion the reorder table above performs on the seven diatonic modes specifically: collapsing circular distance into a flat axis of darkness.

Cadences resolve in the opposite direction - the circle of fifths, toward fewer flats, from tension to resolution. Lydian is so resolved it sits below the root - past rest, into something sweetly ungrounded.

The other thing this view surfaces: the 7th is the most commonly flatted interval because it is the first to go. This is why the 3rd and 7th are called the guide tones - they do the most work of communicating chord quality.

4. Stacking thirds: triads, sevenths, and extensions

A triad is three notes. A seventh chord is four. Beyond those four chord tones, any remaining note of the scale is a suspension - named by its interval from the root: 9th, 11th, 13th. Four chord tones plus three suspensions accounts for all seven notes.

Across all seven modes, there are only three types of triads: major, minor, and diminished. The triad alone cannot distinguish between the three major modes or the three minor modes. You need at least a seventh chord to start hearing the differences.

Mnemonic

The traditional mnemonic for the mode names in scale order is I Don't Play Like A Little Liar - Ionian, Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian, Locrian. It is fine for memorizing the names. It is useless for the sounds.

The circle-of-fourths ordering is more durable because it encodes the structure: Lydian, Ionian, Mixolydian, Dorian, Aeolian, Phrygian, Locrian. Each step is one flat, one step toward darkness.

The 1 is by definition never flatted or sharped in a diatonic mode - it is the starting reference point. The 4 is also never flatted in the standard modes: Lydian sharps it; the others leave it natural.
